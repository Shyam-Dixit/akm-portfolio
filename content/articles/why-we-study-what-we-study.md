Have we ever paused to ask why, from infancy to adulthood, we spend so many years studying? Often, students wonder why they must learn subjects they neither enjoy nor believe will ever be useful. Somewhere along the way, education has become divorced from its purpose, leaving us with degrees that do not always reflect understanding.

Until Class X, students are introduced to a broad range of subjects, each serving a purpose beyond the examination hall.

Language equips us to comprehend, converse and express ourselves with clarity.

Science nurtures curiosity, develops analytical thinking and teaches us to question the world around us.

History helps us understand how societies evolved, how our ancestors lived and the choices they made. Geography gives us an appreciation of the Earth, and our place within a larger world.

Civics prepares us to become responsible citizens who understand both their rights and their responsibilities.

Mathematics is far more than arithmetic. It develops logic and structured thinking

A few decades ago, many schools placed considerable emphasis on mental arithmetic in the primary years. Students were encouraged to solve problems mentally before putting pencil to paper. The objective was not merely speed but the cultivation of concentration and mental agility.

Private tuition existed, but it was generally supplementary rather than an integral part of schooling. The classroom teacher was expected to explain concepts thoroughly and guide students through each stage of learning.

Today, coaching often begins in the primary years. Marks may improve, but genuine understanding does not always keep pace. In the relentless pursuit of examination scores, sports, creative pursuits and other co-curricular activities are frequently pushed aside, even though they are essential to a child's overall development.

At the college level, particularly in the humanities, students are expected to analyse ideas, develop independent opinions and articulate them through discussion and writing. Yet examination-driven learning has increasingly encouraged selective preparation, dependence on ready-made notes and memorisation over original thought. The sciences and engineering, too, have not been entirely immune to this trend.

School and undergraduate education were never intended merely to produce successful examinees. Their larger purpose was to cultivate curious minds, independent thinkers and confident young adults prepared to engage thoughtfully with the world. When learning is reduced to the pursuit of marks alone, that purpose is diminished.
